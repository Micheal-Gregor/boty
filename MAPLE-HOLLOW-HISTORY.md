# Maple Hollow — A Two-Hundred-Year History

*Lore for the world of* Business of the Year. *Original fiction in the small-town-Americana tradition — Wilder's* Our Town*, Anderson's* Winesburg, Ohio*, Keillor's Lake Wobegon, the Bedford Falls of* It's a Wonderful Life*. None of it copied; all of it kin. Dates run to a "today" of roughly 2025, so the founding sits two hundred years back. Every family and storefront named here is one you'll meet across the deck.*

---

## I. The Hollow Before the Town · before 1825 – 1830s

Before it had a name, it had a shape: a sheltered fold in the hills where a cold creek ran fast enough to turn a wheel, and where the sugar maples grew so thick that the first surveyors marked the place on their charts simply as *the maple hollow*. The valley caught the early settlers the way a cupped hand catches rain. The first to stay was a millwright named **Hollis**, who dammed the creek in 1824, raised a sawmill, and discovered that a man who could cut other men's timber would never again want for neighbors.

Where Hollis built, others followed. The Bramble family broke the bottomland for corn. The Tuckers ran cattle on the upper meadows. A Swede named Olafsson kept the first dairy. In late winter the whole settlement turned out to tap the maples and boil the sap down in the snow — the **sugaring**, the oldest tradition the town still keeps, the night the Hollow first learned to work together and split the profit fairly afterward. That argument over the fair split, the old-timers liked to say, was the true founding of Maple Hollow. They were arguing about money before they had a town to spend it in.

## II. Roots Put Down · 1830s – 1860s

A settlement becomes a town when it builds things it can't carry away. The **Grange** came first — the farmers' lodge, half co-op and half church social, where a man's word and his ledger were weighed in the same breath. A chapel went up on the rise, and beside it the Parsonage. **Svenson** opened a proper lumber yard downstream of the mill and never once delivered a board he hadn't been paid for, a habit his great-grandchildren inherited along with the business.

And the trades arrived — quietly, the way they always do. A blacksmith to shoe horses and mend iron (the welders and mechanics would trace their craft to his forge). A pump-and-well man. A tinsmith for the stovepipe. None of them owned much. All of them were indispensable. From the start, Maple Hollow ran on the people who fixed what the town couldn't live without — and on the uneasy truth that being indispensable and being *paid* were two different things.

## III. The Town Named · 1867

In the spring of 1867 the settlement filed its charter and became, officially, **Maple Hollow — Est. 1867**, the date still cut into the lintel over the hardware store. Three things happened almost at once, and together they made a town out of a cluster of farms.

The **rail spur** reached the valley, a branch line ending at a new depot, and suddenly Hollis lumber could go to market and the world could come to the Hollow. **First Hollow Bank** opened its doors under a cautious, decent man named **Folsom** — the first of the line — who lent against character as much as collateral and kept the town's money close to home. And the county raised a **Courthouse** and a **Town Hall** on the square, which is how the **Crabtrees** got into politics and never left. By the time the bunting came down, Maple Hollow had a Main Street, a charter, and opinions about everything.

## IV. The Bright Years · 1870s – 1920s

This was the town the postcards remembered. The **Hollis Mill** ran three shifts and its whistle set the rhythm of every day. Money moved, and Main Street rose to meet it: **Hollow Hardware**, the creamery, the barbershop, a diner that the town would one day know simply as **Dot's**. The merchants, grown prosperous and a little vain, built an **Opera House** far grander than the Hollow strictly needed, and were proud of it for a hundred years. The **Odd Fellows** raised their lodge hall; the **Grange Hall** became the town's living room, where the vendor fairs and the harvest dances and the heated meetings all happened under the same tin ceiling.

As the town wired itself for electric light and plumbed itself for running water, the trades multiplied — electricians, pipefitters, plumbers, the men who climbed the new poles and crawled the new trenches. Out on the ridge the **Vales** planted a vineyard and put on airs. The **County Fair** drew the whole region. And the merchants, tired of being cheated by drummers and undercut by sharps, founded the **Maple Hollow Better Business Bureau** to police fair dealing among themselves — and, every winter, to hang a ribbon on the one shop that had done the year proudest. They called it **Business of the Year**. The first winner was a wheelwright. The losers, naturally, the **Pettigrews**, have been trying to win it ever since.

## V. The Long Test · 1930s – 1970s

Then the country fell, and the Hollow learned what it was made of. In the Depression the mill cut to one shift and First Hollow Bank came within a single bad week of closing its doors — until a Folsom stood at the teller's window and personally talked the town out of a panic, lending out his own savings to keep the doors open. People remembered. They are still, three generations later, polite to the Folsoms in a way they are polite to no one else.

The farms and the trades carried the town through. You cannot eat a stock certificate, but you can eat what the Brambles grow, and you can keep the lights on if the electrician will run you credit. Two wars took the young men; some came home in uniform and traded it for coveralls, and that GI generation — **Old Vern Tucker** chief among them — built the postwar Hollow with their hands. A proper **Fire Hall** went up, and the **Boons** have answered its bell ever since. These were quiet, enduring decades, and they taught the town its hardest and most useful lesson: that a business can look healthy on paper and still die for want of cash in the drawer. Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. Maple Hollow has believed this, in its bones, ever since.

## VI. The Lean Decades · 1980s – 2000s

Every mill town has the year the whistle stops, and for Maple Hollow it came late and hard. The **Hollis Mill** — the heart of the place for a century and a half — went quiet for good, its last logs cut and its great rooms left to the pigeons. The rail depot followed it into silence. The young people followed the rail out of town. Storefronts on Main Street went dark one by one; the Opera House was boarded; the County Fair shrank to a midway and a raffle.

Into that vacuum stepped the **Pettigrew brothers**, who understood that a desperate town pays a man who shows up, whether or not he does it right. They worked fast, bid low, cut every corner that didn't show, and grew — and the better tradespeople gritted their teeth and watched. But the Hollow did not die. **Dot** kept the diner lit and the coffee hot and the gossip flowing, because a town that still gathers somewhere is still a town. **Svenson** kept the gate of the yard open. The Grange still met, even if half the chairs were empty. The Hollow held on out of pure stubbornness, waiting — though it could not yet have said for what.

## VII. The Turn · 2010s – Today

What it was waiting for, it turned out, was an idea — and the idea came from the Better Business Bureau. Under its sharp-eyed chair **Eunice Vale**, the Bureau dusted off the old **Business of the Year** ribbon and made it something more than a merchant's vanity: a public contest to see who could rebuild the most of the town, one honest job at a time. The trades, it argued, were not what was left of Maple Hollow. The trades were how it would come back.

And they have. The Odd Fellows hall is rewired and rented out for weddings. The **old Hollis Mill** is being retrofitted into a warehouse — steel and pipe and big money — its ruin made useful again. The town raised a new firehouse for **Chief Boon**. There is talk, financed and dangerous, of fitting out the Hollow's first three-story office tower. Outside money has noticed: a smooth, impatient **Developer** who builds big and pays slow, and a quiet **Newcomer** who bought the long-empty Vance place and keeps to himself, and about whom the diner has not yet made up its mind.

It is a town steeped in tradition and itching for the future, optimistic and cutthroat in the same breath, where everyone has known everyone for a hundred years and competes with them anyway. The mill whistle is silent, but Main Street is louder than it has been in a generation — full of trucks and ladders and the particular music of a place fixing itself up. The Hollow is open for business. It always was.

## The Town Today — who's who

The cast you meet across the deck are the living end of these two hundred years:

- **Mayor Crabtree** — the Crabtrees have run the square since 1867; politics is the family trade. Wants his legacy built and isn't fussy about how.
- **Dwight Folsom** — the banker, heir to the man who saved the town. Lends safe, calls shaky loans, and is owed a century of deference.
- **Dot** — the diner, the gossip, the warm beating heart of Main Street. Her good word is worth more than the bank's.
- **Eunice Vale** — Bureau chair and vineyard heiress; architect of the revival and keeper of the ribbon.
- **Sven Svenson** — the lumber yard, third generation, paid on time or not at all.
- **Old Man Hettrick** — old family, old money, and not parting with a nickel of it; disputes every bill on principle.
- **Inspector Grit** — code enforcement, incorruptible, and quietly delighted to find you out of spec.
- **Marge Tolliver** — the assessor, who can make your shop worth more for the tax roll with a single visit.
- **Hal Ramsey** — Chamber of Commerce and Hollow Hardware; relentlessly upbeat, forever organizing.
- **Chief Boon** — the firehouse, the Boons' long watch; when he says *now*, he means it.
- **The Developer** — the outsider with the blueprints and the slow checkbook.
- **The Newcomer** — the wildcard at the old Vance place. Time will tell.
- **The Pettigrew brothers** — the rivals, still bidding low and cutting corners, still chasing the ribbon that has eluded them since the very first year.
- **Old Vern**, lately retired, who built half of what the rest of them are now fixing.

Two hundred years on, the argument is the same one they had over the sugaring snow: who did the work, who gets paid, and who did the town proudest this year. Welcome to Maple Hollow. Mind the ledger.
